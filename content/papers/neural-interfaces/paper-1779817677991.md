### Machine Learning Assisted Optimization Framework for Designing Transcranial Focused Ultrasound Phased Array Transducer for Deep Brain Neuromodulation in Mice

**Core engineering problem:**  
Designing a bowl‑shaped phased‑array transducer that can reliably focus ultrasound energy at a deep brain target (hypothalamus) in mice, despite severe skull‑base attenuation and focal distortion. The challenge is to select operating frequency, element count, and element diameter that jointly maximize focal intensity, minimize fragmentation and displacement, all while keeping the transducer compact and manufacturable.

**Methodology & tech stack:**  
- Full‑wave acoustic simulations on a computed‑tomography (CT)‑derived mouse head model.  
- Fixed geometry (10 mm aperture, 6 mm radius of curvature) with variable parameters: frequency 0.2–1.5 MHz, active elements 16–128, element diameter 300–550 µm.  
- Four performance metrics: presence of a –3 dB focal region in the hypothalamus, axial full‑width‑at‑half‑maximum (FWHM), focal fragmentation (blob count), and targeting displacement.  
- Random Forest surrogate models trained on simulation outputs to approximate the mapping from design parameters to metrics.  
- Non‑Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA‑II) coupled with the surrogates for efficient multi‑objective optimization, drastically reducing full‑wave simulation calls.  
- Comparison with time‑reversal phase conjugation and a single‑element baseline.  
- Hardware realization concept: a 128‑element array operating at 0.73 MHz with 381 µm diameter elements.

**Biomedical innovation:**  
This work delivers a data‑driven, computationally efficient framework for tailoring focused‑ultrasound transducers to deep‑brain targets in small animals. The optimized design yields a tightly confined focal spot (FWHM ≈ 0.67 mm) and minimal off‑target fragmentation, enabling precise neuromodulation of the hypothalamus. Such a platform accelerates preclinical studies of non‑invasive brain stimulation, informs transducer scaling for larger mammals, and demonstrates how machine‑learning surrogates can replace expensive physics simulations in device design.
